# Discover scenery (prototype)

Discover's main map shows illustrated scenery in an adventure-atlas style:
- the Alps as continuous painted relief made from real elevation data;
- mountain groups along the other real ranges;
- gently irregular hills on lower ranges;
- small tree clumps in real forests;
- a few wave marks at sea.

It is a visual prototype. It is drawn in Discover only, never in Find, Travel or the Luxembourg close-up.

Code: geographic data in `src/geo/terrain.ts`; the Alpine relief rasters in `src/assets/map/alps-relief*` (made by `scripts/generate-alps-relief.mjs`); artwork (plain path and circle data) in `src/components/map/sceneryArt.ts`; rendering in `src/components/map/Scenery.tsx` (+ `Scenery.module.css`); tests in `src/geo/terrain.test.ts` and `e2e/scenery.spec.ts`.

## Atlas surface (Discover, prototype at 390px)

Discover's map has a bright illustrated-atlas surface; Find and Travel keep the earlier palette.
- **Colours.**
  - Water is turquoise, with a light shallow-water edge along every coast (a wide pale stroke under the land).
  - Land is fresh green with clean white borders.
  - Neighbouring countries belong to the same green landscape, a little paler and with softer borders.
  - A selected country is teal: greener and deeper than the sea, and set off from it by the white border and coastal edge.
  - Tokens are in `.atlas` in `src/components/map/RegionMap.module.css`.
- **Texture.**
  - Two small tileable WebP textures (`src/assets/map/`, about 29 KB together), generated by `scripts/generate-map-textures.mjs` from periodic value noise:
    - soft, irregular light and deep patches for the land;
    - faint streaks and mottling for the water.
  - They are decorative colour variation only, not elevation or land cover.
  - They are painted as SVG patterns in world coordinates (`src/components/map/AtlasSurface.tsx`), so they stay anchored while zooming and panning.
  - Land texture is filled into the country paths themselves, so it follows the real coastlines and borders; over a selected country it is at 35%.
  - Textures never take pointer events.
- **Scenery.**
  - Brighter palettes: green trees with highlights, grey-stone mountains with white snow.
  - The relief wash along ranges and the grass tufts are gone; the land texture replaces them.
- **Unchanged.** The close-up keeps flat colours without texture or scenery.

## Alpine relief (prototype)

The Alps are no longer a row of mountain symbols. They are one continuous piece of relief painted into the land, made from real elevation data. The other ranges still use symbols; the Alps are the first range converted.

- **What it looks like.**
  - Green foothills fade into the surrounding land, then olive and grey-green middle slopes, muted grey rock, and snow only on the highest massifs (most visibly Mont Blanc and the Valais Alps).
  - One light, from the north-west (upper left), lights every slope, so ridges and valleys read as height.
  - Valleys, lake basins and plains stay the land's own green; the relief has no outline, baseline or platform.
  - It runs across the French, Swiss, Italian, German, Austrian and Slovenian borders as one landscape. Borders are drawn on top, as separate lines.
- **Selection.** Over a selected (teal) country, a teal version of the same relief is drawn instead: teal foothills, sea-green and mint slopes, white snow, with the same light and shadow. The white name and badge still read first.
- **How it is made** (`scripts/generate-alps-relief.mjs`, run once; its output is committed):
  1. Elevation tiles at zoom 8 (about 400 m per pixel here) are downloaded and cached.
  2. The elevation is resampled into the map's own projected "world" coordinates, with the same projection as the map.
  3. It is smoothed. The whole-map level keeps only broad ridges and valleys (about 2.4 km smoothing), since a 390px phone shows the Alps about 220px wide. The zoomed level keeps more of the real valleys (about 1.1 km).
  4. Hillshade (light from 315°, 40° high; vertical exaggeration ×5 and ×3.2) and elevation colour zones (smoothed further, so the zones are broad) are painted into an RGBA raster.
  5. Opacity fades in from about 450 m to 1,100 m, so the foothills blend into the land; slopes show a little lower down.
  6. Relief is painted only inside Natural Earth's *ALPS* range polygon, fading out over 28 km outside it, so its coarse outline never shows. Elevation shapes the rest. The Jura, Black Forest, Apennines and Dinaric ranges are outside it and keep their symbols.
- **How it is drawn** (`AlpineRelief` in `src/components/map/Scenery.tsx`):
  - An SVG `<image>` at a fixed world position, inside the same transformed group as the countries, so it stays anchored while zooming and panning. `src/assets/map/alps-relief.json` records the position; a test confirms its control points (Mont Blanc, Triglav, Zugspitze) match the map's projection.
  - It is clipped to land (selected and other countries separately, like the symbols) and drawn after the land texture, before trees, symbols and borders.
  - From zoom scale 0.6 (about 1.7× the whole-map view on a phone) the detailed level is drawn over the simplified one. It is fetched only then, and the simplified one stays underneath while it loads.
  - It takes no pointer events: taps reach the country underneath.
  - Files: 41 KB each for the whole-map level (normal and teal), about 200 KB each for the detailed level.
- **Names.** The relief is a surface, not a symbol, so it doesn't take part in label layout, and names are never moved for it. At the views checked (390px whole map, zoomed and deep-zoomed, in English and Armenian; 1366px whole map), no lesson name or capital sits on it.

## How it is drawn

- **Artwork.** A small family of reusable SVG symbols, each defined once and placed with `<use>`:
  - three mountain groups: a three-peak massif, a twin-peak ridge and a lone peak with a shoulder. They have irregular silhouettes and varied heights, a lit face and a shaded face, and restrained snow on the high summits only. The Pyrenees use them with snow (the Alps now use relief instead, see above); rocky ranges (Apennines, Cantabrian, Dinaric, Grampians) use the same silhouettes lower and without snow;
  - two hill shapes: overlapping mounds of different heights, and a low rolling line;
  - two broadleaf clumps and two conifer clumps, with trees of different heights and canopy shapes and two-tone shading;
  - a wave.

  They follow the landmark illustrations: two-tone fills and a soft ink outline along the skyline only, never along the ground. Each placement picks a variant, a size (±12%) and a mirror deterministically, so the same place always looks the same. There are no images or fonts, and no new mapping library; the scenery uses the existing d3 projection and zoom.
- **Composition.**
  - Mountain and hill groups are spaced about a group's width apart along each crest. They may overlap at their foothills, forming a chain, but never stack: a group whose core would cover another group's is left out.
  - Every crest also has a group at its middle, so short ranges keep one on small maps.
  - Tree clumps keep at least 12px apart (15px on phones) and clear of mountain groups; a forest never spreads past its radius. Grass keeps clear of everything else.
- **Geographic anchoring.**
  - Every symbol is anchored at a projected world position inside the same transformed group as the countries, so it stays on its place while zooming and panning.
  - Symbols keep a constant size on screen; one CSS variable (`--inv` = 1/zoom) undoes the zoom scale.
  - Level of detail uses nested grids and fixed-phase spacing along ridges, so zooming in *adds* symbols between the existing ones and never moves them.
- **Layer order.**
  - Country fills come first, then the land texture, the Alpine relief and the scenery symbols, then the borders, drawn again on top.
  - After that come the overlay's route, markers and names.
  - Scenery has `pointer-events: none`, so taps and gestures reach the country underneath.
- **Clipping.**
  - Land symbols are clipped to land and waves to the sea (sea = coverage rectangle minus land, even-odd).
  - Before that, each symbol's footprint is checked to fit on land at the current zoom. Trees and hills must fit inside one country with 3px to spare, so they keep a little room from borders and coasts. Mountain groups may cross borders, since many crests are borders.
- **Space for names.**
  - Scenery is placed after the label layout and gives way to it. Any symbol that would touch these is left out:
    - a name or its explored badge, with 6px clearance;
    - a callout or leader line;
    - a capital or landmark marker, or its name;
    - the close-up's dashed area;
    - Luxembourg's outline, with 12px clearance;
    - the zoom controls and the close-up button or panel, with 8px of room.
  - Names are never moved or hidden for decoration.
- **Low Countries.** Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg get no scenery at all.
- **Selection.** Over a selected (teal) country the same artwork is redrawn in a teal palette: mint stone and deep sea-green trees and hills. Land is split with clip paths into selected and other countries. A group standing across the border (the Pyrenees, the French Alps) is drawn in each palette on its own side of the border. It stays recognisable, while the white name, the check badge and the capital marker read first. On faded neighbours scenery is at 80% opacity.
- **Phones.** Maps narrower than 420px use each range's main crest only. They also get somewhat wider spacing for mountain groups and hills, fewer waves and 84% symbol size.
- **Motion.** None; the scenery is static, so reduced motion needs no special case.

The Natural Earth check covers 13 ranges and 89 crest points. It is optional because the Natural Earth file isn't part of the repo:

```bash
NE_REGIONS=path/to/regions.geojson npx vitest run src/geo/terrain.test.ts
```

Result:
- 85 of 89 points lie inside their named range polygon.
- The other 4 lie within 3–14 km of it, all real summits just outside Natural Earth's coarse outlines: Triglav (Alps), the Apennines' north-western end, the Vosges' southern end and Snowdon (Cambrian).

Three ranges are anchored only on Wikipedia summits:
- **Black Forest and Swabian Jura:** Natural Earth has no polygon for them.
- **Grampians:** Natural Earth's Scottish polygon ("Cruach nam Miseag") is a thin strip along the Highland edge that misses Ben Nevis by 37 km.

### Forests

Each forest is a centre (the Wikipedia article's coordinate) and an approximate radius:

- **France:** Landes forest, Compiègne, Morvan, Vosges.
- **Germany:** Black Forest, Palatinate Forest, Odenwald, Spessart, Rothaar Mountains, Teutoburg Forest, Solling, Harz, Thuringian Forest, Bavarian Forest, Schorfheide.
- **Neighbours, shown faded:** New Forest, Thetford, Kielder and Galloway (Great Britain), and the Tuchola Forest (Poland).

The tests check that each centre lies in its stated country (`src/geo/terrain.test.ts`). Trees thin out towards the edge of the radius, and the centre always has a clump.

### Waves

The 14 wave marks sit at named open-sea points (Bay of Biscay, North Sea, Gulf of Lion, Adriatic…). The tests check that each is at least 25 km from any coast.

## Simplifications and limitations

- **Alpine relief is simplified, and artistic.**
  - Real elevation is smoothed (2.4 km, or 1.1 km zoomed in) and its height exaggerated, so small valleys and individual peaks merge.
  - Colours follow smoothed elevation, not land cover: forest, pasture, rock and glaciers are not mapped. "Snow" is simply the highest smoothed ground (from about 2,600–2,850 m), not the real glacier extent.
  - Lakes (Geneva, Constance, Garda…) aren't in the map data, so they show as flat land.
  - The light direction is a cartographic convention (north-west), not the sun's.
  - At the deepest zoom (8×) the detailed raster is softer than the borders.
- **Other ranges are schematic, not measured.**
  - Ridges follow a few summits, and forests are circles.
  - Their real outlines, land cover and elevation are not used.
- **Two styles side by side.** Until the other ranges are converted, the Pyrenees, Apennines, Dinaric Alps and others still use mountain symbols, which look like separate objects next to the Alpine relief (most visible on desktop).
  - At the whole-map view the scenery is a pictorial hint of where mountains and woods are, not an exact extent.
- **Left out deliberately:**
  - the Ardennes, Eifel and Oesling (Low Countries and Luxembourg clearance);
  - the Veluwe;
  - the Carpathians, Scandinavian mountains and other ranges that rarely come into view.
- **Names win.**
  - Where a name sits on a forest or range, that scenery is not drawn. For example, "Germany" covers the Thuringian Forest, and Luxembourg's callout covers the Palatinate Forest, Odenwald and Spessart on wide maps.
  - Small forests may show a single clump at the whole-map view.
- **Sparse trees on phones.** Trees appear only in the real forests listed below, so a 390px map shows just a few clumps. Matching the reference's scattered trees would need invented forests, which this prototype avoids.
- **Schorfheide.** No clump showed at the whole-map views checked (1366, 390 and 320px). Its centre's footprint did not pass the land check there; behaviour when zoomed in is not verified.
- **Performance.**
  - Checked only in desktop Chromium with 4× CPU throttling, zooming and panning at 390px and 1366px.
  - Median and 95th-percentile frame times were the same with the scenery shown or hidden. At 1366px the single worst frame was up to about 30ms longer with it shown (150–167ms against 133–150ms).
  - Not measured on physical phones.
